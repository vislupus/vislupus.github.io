I had always dreamed of visiting China, but I kept thinking it was too far away and that getting there would be very hard. It took Marco Polo years to get there and back. Yes, these days we have planes, but it is still the other side of the world. At the beginning of November, though, I decided I had to do it – I grew up on Shaolin and kung fu movies and I simply had to go, however far it was. After a few days of digging through the programs of different travel agencies, I chose one that included as many of the places I wanted to see as possible. All that was left was to wait half a year – but that also meant half a year of dreaming about what it would be like.

And it was amazing. We spent 11 days in China – from 3 to 13 May 2026 – and passed through Beijing, Shaolin, Xi'an, Guilin and Shanghai. It is one of those places I want to return to many times, and I will definitely keep dreaming about it for a long time.

::: curious
**China in a few numbers**

- About 1.4 billion people – for a long time the most populous country in the world, until India overtook it in 2023.
- 9.6 million km² – almost 87 times the size of Bulgaria.
- 56 officially recognised ethnic groups, of which the Han make up over 91%.
- The whole country lives in a single time zone (UTC+8), even though it spans five from east to west. Compared to Bulgaria, that means a 6-hour difference in winter and 5 hours in summer.
:::


## Before you go

Before I start the story, here are a few things that are good to know if you also decide to head to China.

::: warning
**Drugs and gambling.** China's laws against drugs and gambling are extremely strict. Possession and use of drugs are punished severely, and serious crimes involving trafficking, production or distribution can even lead to the death penalty.

Gambling is also banned in most of China, and breaking the rules can lead to heavy fines and prison. An interesting exception is Macau, where casinos are legal and the gambling industry is highly developed.
:::

::: warning
**Exit bans.** In China it is possible that, when you try to leave the country, you are refused exit if you have unresolved legal or financial issues with the local authorities or with citizens.

This can happen with unpaid debts that have led to a court dispute, pending lawsuits, investigations or other serious violations of the law. In such cases the authorities can impose an exit ban until the problem is resolved or the restriction is lifted.

The worst part is that sometimes people don't even know there is such a ban until they reach the airport. And solving the problem can take months, sometimes years.
:::

::: tip
**Internet and apps.** In China, Western websites and apps (Google, WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, etc.) don't work without a VPN or a SIM/eSIM card from a foreign operator. Keep in mind that even with these it depends on your phone – they may still not work, so check in advance what you need, or get an eSIM compatible with your phone on the spot.

Practically everyone I talked to messaged through WeChat. It is something like Viber or WhatsApp, but with far more features – the Chinese use it for almost everything, including payments.

For Alipay and WeChat Pay you need internet. Since 2023 both apps allow you to link a foreign Visa or Mastercard card, and for smaller payments (under 200 yuan) there is usually no fee. In practice, though, our cards didn't always go through, and some features (for example transfers between people) require a Chinese bank account.
:::

::: tip
**Carry some cash too.** You may end up somewhere where there is no other way to pay. You often read online that paper money is no longer used in China, but that's not true – I saw plenty of locals paying in cash.
:::

::: warning
**Always have your passport on you.** In China you must always carry your passport, because the police can ask to check it at any time. On top of that, entry to almost all museums and sights is with a passport.
:::

::: note
**A bit about money.** 100 Chinese yuan (CNY) is about 13.4 euros (at the November 2026 exchange rate). For comparison – the average private-sector wage in Chinese cities in 2025 was around €800 a month, while in Bulgaria in the second quarter of 2026 it was €1,444, i.e. about 80% higher.

If you look at the average disposable income per person in the villages – including children, pensioners, income from farming and other sources – it is about €273 a month.

How much cheaper life in China is depends a lot on who you ask – cost-of-living comparison sites give wildly different results: according to some, China is about a quarter cheaper than Bulgaria, according to others it is even slightly more expensive. By those numbers, big cities like Beijing and Shanghai come out roughly on par with Sofia, while smaller cities are noticeably cheaper.
:::


## Beijing

After a whole night on the plane we finally landed in Beijing. We were met by a group of staff who directed us where to queue, and we immediately felt something that would be our constant companion throughout the trip – order. Later we saw it at the airports and the train stations too: in China everything works like a well-oiled machine and everything happens exactly when it should – the complete opposite of what we saw on departure at Istanbul airport.

At first it felt strange that police officers stood there watching us and that there were cameras everywhere, but after a few days we got used to it and stopped noticing. One thing I never understood, though, is how scooters are allowed to fly along the roads and pavements. I had the feeling that there were no rules for them and that they were above the law. I guess it is because they have to deliver their parcels on time, but even so, the way they ride is not normal. Still, the situation is many times better than in Thailand.

On the first evening I decided to walk around the hotel, and what surprised me most was how modern and at the same time how traditional China is. On one side of the boulevard there may be a huge mall with expensive cars out front, and on the other – a traditional medicine shop. At first this surprised me, but over the next days I saw it everywhere and got used to it.

### Tiananmen Square

The day started very early, because we were told we had to get to the square as early as possible – even being 10 minutes late could later cost us hours of waiting in queues. We managed to get in on time, when there weren't many people yet, but really just 20 minutes after us it turned into madness, and we were grateful we had come early.

::: warning
There are cameras and police everywhere in China, and everything that happens on the streets and in buildings is monitored. This is most visible right on Tiananmen Square.
:::

The square is the meeting point of power in China. If you go clockwise from the Forbidden City (to the north), next come the National Museum (to the east), Mao's Mausoleum (to the south) and the Great Hall of the People (to the west), where the Chinese parliament meets. The name Tiananmen means "Gate of Heavenly Peace" – that is the name of the gate to the Forbidden City, on which hangs the famous portrait of Mao. It is precisely because of the symbolism of the place that the protests of spring 1989 took place here.

What really struck me was how many Chinese people were there and almost no foreigners. Over the following days the picture was the same almost everywhere – around 80–90% of the visitors at the sights were locals. Because of the huge distances in China, even people from other provinces are like visitors from another country, and for them a visit to the capital is a real experience.

We were warned not to photograph the police and the military, because it is forbidden. However tempting it is, don't do it.

One moment there really touched me. While I was walking around taking photos, a boy of about 6 came up to me, and after a few attempts at understanding each other it turned out he wanted a photo with me. It had never happened to me before that a local person wanted to take a picture with me – and in the very centre of China at that. I had heard that people from the remote, non-touristy parts of the country rarely see foreigners and that the appearance of one is an event, but I had never experienced it. It wasn't the only time – later on, people would just come up to me and ask for a photo together.

One of the questions I'm asked most often is what Chinese people are like. Honestly, I came away with very good impressions. Probably because I'm a foreigner and they tried to be hospitable, but to me the Chinese are good people who live in a fairly harsh environment, who have historically been trampled on and humiliated by many, but have kept their humanity. Of course, the population is huge and there will always be someone who wants to cheat a foreigner and profit at their expense, but on the whole they are kind and welcoming people.

### The Forbidden City

We have seen the Forbidden City many times in films, but in reality few films have actually been shot there. The best known is "The Last Emperor" (1987) – the first Western feature film allowed to shoot inside the palace. Today most productions are filmed elsewhere, and the palace is added with computer graphics, or realistic sets are used – in Hengdian, "China's Hollywood", there is an entire full-size copy of the Forbidden City. And if you think being an actor in China is great, think again: we were told that many actors (especially extras) act in films and series during the day and deliver food in the evening, because it is hard to make a living from this profession alone.

The palace itself was built between 1406 and 1420 under the Yongle Emperor of the Ming dynasty, and for almost 500 years it was home to the emperors of the Ming and Qing dynasties. It is called "forbidden" because without the emperor's permission nobody could either enter or leave.

::: curious
If you look closely, you will see that the halls are almost empty – without decoration and with almost no furniture. We were told that the reason is this: in 1948–1949, during the Chinese Civil War, Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government moved thousands of valuable objects from the imperial collections to Taiwan. Today they are kept in the National Palace Museum in Taipei.

They didn't take everything, though – of around 13,000 crates evacuated during the wars, 2,972 went to Taiwan. The museum in the Forbidden City still holds over 1.8 million objects today; most of them are simply in storage and in the exhibition galleries rather than in the palace halls.
:::

Something that caught my eye was that the paving in many places is full of holes, the paint on the buildings is peeling, and I wouldn't be surprised if a rotten plank was hanging somewhere. We were told that they deliberately don't renovate the Forbidden City so that it looks authentic. Still, it's strange for one of China's biggest attractions to be dangerous to walk around, with you constantly looking at your feet so you don't break something in a hole. Later I found out that the palace is in fact being restored, but selectively – they try to preserve as much of the original material as possible, and because of its enormous size they simply can't repair everything at once.

What impressed me most, though, were the girls dressed in traditional clothes of princesses and empresses, walking around and taking photos everywhere. At times I felt as if we had been transported several centuries back – to the time when princesses really did walk here. Only now they are looking for the perfect spot for a social media photo.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260504_095605.jpg", "Girls in Qing-era costumes in the Forbidden City" %}

Over the following days I saw girls in traditional clothes almost everywhere, and the interesting thing was that the costumes changed with the region. In the Forbidden City they were mostly in the style of the last dynasty, the Qing, in Xi'an – in the style of the Tang dynasty, and around Guilin – in the costumes of the local ethnic minorities.

If you are interested in what life in the imperial palace was like, I recommend the anime "The Apothecary Diaries". It is set in a fictional empire inspired mainly by the Tang era, so it is not historically accurate, but it shows very well how people lived in the palace and what intrigues went on there. If you want something set specifically in the Forbidden City, watch the series "Story of Yanxi Palace" – it takes place in the time of the Qianlong Emperor of the Qing dynasty.

### Pearl factory

When you're told you will visit a "factory" in China, imagine that you will be taken to a shop, probably in a mall, where prices will be quite inflated because – as they assured us – the state guarantees the quality.

That was exactly our experience. We were taken to a shop and first given a long explanation of how great, incredible and valuable the pearls are and how we absolutely had to buy some. In these shops bargaining isn't common, but they may agree to some kind of discount.

The pearls really are very beautiful, but they are now produced in huge quantities and most are cheap. In many places in China you will be promised genuine pearls, and in most cases they will be fakes or low-quality pearls sold to you as something very rare. If you still want to buy pearls, a shop like this is the safer place.

### The Olympic Park

After all these sights, our guide had prepared something special and took us to the Olympic Park, built for the 2008 Games. That's where the iconic buildings we all remember from the Games are: the National Stadium, known as the "Bird's Nest", the National Aquatics Center – the "Water Cube", and the Linglong Tower, which housed the TV studios during the Games. A little further on rises the 258-metre Olympic Tower, opened in 2015. Overall it's a great place for a walk, but things look deceptively close while in reality the distances are quite large. Definitely worth a visit.

I had heard that the Chinese consider the number 4 unlucky because it sounds almost like the word for "death", but I didn't believe that some hotels have no fourth floor or rooms with the number 4 at all. It turned out to be true, though – in Shanghai the lift really had no button for floor 4.

The number 8, on the other hand, symbolises wealth, success and prosperity, because its pronunciation resembles the word for "getting rich". That's why the Beijing Olympics opened on 08.08.2008 at exactly 20:08.

### Beijing Zoo

After the nice walk we wanted to see real pandas, so we went to Beijing Zoo, which has several pandas and many other animals. Overall it is a pleasant place for a walk, but I wouldn't plan it specially unless you want to see pandas – there are no truly exotic animals that can't be seen elsewhere in the world, and it isn't very big. For me it was a bit of a disappointment: I couldn't get close to a panda, the two I saw were behind glass, and after 30 minutes in a queue we were told we were too late and the pandas were already asleep.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260504_153258.jpg", "A panda enjoying all the attention" %}

::: curious
Almost all giant pandas in foreign zoos are owned by China. Other countries can't simply buy a panda – they have to sign a special agreement, usually for 10 years. Zoos pay around 1 million dollars a year for a pair of pandas, and cubs born abroad also remain the property of China and are sent back there when they are 2–4 years old.

Pandas also play an important role in diplomacy. China uses them as a symbol of friendship and good relations, and if relations sour, it may refuse to renew the agreement or ask for the animals back – a decision seen as a serious diplomatic signal.

So these lovable black-and-white bears turn out to be not just a tourist attraction, but China's ambassadors around the world.
:::

### Peking duck dinner

One of the things that impressed me most was the way the Chinese eat. Traditionally there are many dishes on the table and everyone takes a bit of everything. Nowadays people rarely have time for this, but on holidays, when relatives gather from all over the country, many dishes are still cooked and a big feast is laid out.

Many people, however, now prefer to order food online – it's delivered in minutes, so you can put together a traditional feast with almost no effort, and the most interesting thing is that the price is quite reasonable. That's why many simply can't cook, and probably don't want to either. In general, almost everything in China can be ordered online.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260504_175912.jpg", "A traditional table laden with dishes" %}

If you imagine Peking duck as a stuffed duck or a duck in some strange sweet-and-sour sauce, you're quite wrong. The star here is the crispy skin – the duck is dried, glazed and roasted in a special way, then sliced into thin pieces. It's served with small thin pancakes, vegetables and a wonderful dark sauce: you put a slice of duck and some vegetables in a pancake, roll it up, dip it in the sauce – and it tastes amazing, but it's quite greasy and heavy.

::: curious
The Chinese in general eat much less sugar than we do. Their desserts taste almost bland to us, so don't be surprised if you bring Chinese sweets home and everyone tells you they're not sweet at all – that's simply part of their cuisine.
:::

### Chaoyang Park

Usually after a long day full of experiences you want to lie down and rest, but I decided to go to a park that seemed very close to the hotel. Except that distances in China are huge, and this "little park nearby" turned out to be 5 km away. On the other hand, it gave me the chance to see what the locals do in the evening.

In the park there were people jogging or walking their dogs – nothing unusual. As I was walking, though, I saw something glowing in the sky. I thought it was a drone, but it turned out to be a kite with little lights along its string, just glowing and floating in the sky. It turned out to be a fairly popular hobby – I'd seen one in the Olympic Park too, and over the next days as well.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260504_201018.jpg", "A glowing kite in the night sky" %}

At one point I heard music and decided to see what was going on. I expected something like the Singing Fountains in Plovdiv, but it turned out to be something much more interesting – a group of young people had gathered by the lake and were singing. I thought there was a concert, but there was no stage and no performer – just people singing together. After a long search for someone who spoke English (and that is a hard task in China), I found a girl who explained that the day before, at the "Bird's Nest" stadium, there had been a concert by the Taiwanese rock band Mayday. The band is hugely popular in China, but I had never heard of them.

{% video "images/china/VID_20260504_203510.mp4", { caption: "Young people singing their favourite song" } %}

She also explained what the strange glowing balls in the lake were, which I'd been wondering about too. It turned out they were the mascots of the band members – each colour stands for one of the five musicians. Later I found out that the giant balloons appear in the park's lake for their concerts (they were first put there in summer 2025), and this time they had been set up about two weeks before we arrived. In May 2026 Mayday played no fewer than 12 concerts in Beijing, attended by nearly 650,000 people.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260504_203155.jpg", "The five glowing balloons in the lake – one for each member of Mayday" %}

I spent several hours talking to this girl – about her life and mine and about the differences between China and Bulgaria. What surprised me most was how wrong an idea I had had about China. I imagined it as an absolute dictatorship where people are crushed, and if someone says something against those in power, they are thrown in prison or killed in the street. These young people made me change my mind. Yes, such things exist in China, but in my opinion they are not as common or as brutal as television shows them. While we were talking, I thought about how many misconceptions we in the West have about China and about Asia as a whole. These countries definitely need to be visited, to see first-hand what is happening there, rather than just listening on TV to how terrible a place China is – because at least my impression is that this isn't true.

A few days later I shared my impressions with one of our guides, who had travelled a lot around Europe. I told her that at first I was worried about the cameras and the police, but after a few days I stopped noticing them and felt completely safe walking alone at night – something that would be almost unthinkable in some Italian cities. I also asked her about the social credit score. She said they really do have one, but it doesn't worry them unless it drops a lot. I was shocked, because I expected her to criticise it, and for her it was something normal. I also noticed that the Chinese don't like to talk about those in power and the various systems, and when they do, they never criticise them. That may be the reason for her opinion, or perhaps they really don't care and see it like a credit score in America.

::: note
**What it turned out to be:** when I read up on it later, I learned that a single national system that gives points to every citizen doesn't exist. The so-called social credit system is more a collection of registers and "blacklists" – mainly for companies and for people who don't comply with court decisions (for example, don't pay their debts), and these people may face restrictions, for example when buying plane tickets. Some cities have tried local points systems, but participation in them is voluntary. So the comparison with a credit score isn't entirely wrong. This doesn't mean there is no mass surveillance – the cameras I saw everywhere are very real.
:::

### The Great Wall of China

The day started with a long drive to Juyongguan – a pass about 55–60 km north-west of central Beijing. I was very excited, because this was one of the places I most wanted to see in China. The only thing that worried me was that we would have very little time – about 2 hours, which, however I looked at it, wasn't going to be enough. But I had to make do with what I had.

We got off the bus and people immediately went shopping – as if the wall didn't matter, only the next souvenirs. And of course to get a certificate saying they had been on the wall. It felt strange to me, because most of them probably wouldn't even reach the highest point, but they would have a certificate.

What nobody told us at the time is that Juyongguan is a fortified pass – one of the key defensive positions against armies coming from the north. The location was chosen very cleverly: the wall climbs up both mountain slopes, with a narrow pass between them, so the defenders could control passage through the valley. There is even a saying that here one man can hold the pass against ten thousand enemies – an exaggeration that shows how strong the natural defences were.

All we were told was that the climb to the top takes about an hour, and the descent about the same. Nobody mentioned that you can do a full loop or how long it takes – just that the start and the end are in the same place. And with this minimal information, the climb began.

The wall is made of stone and in places the steps are quite steep. Along it are towers where soldiers were stationed. This section has been restored and doesn't look exactly as it did in ancient times – in fact, much of the wall was destroyed and rebuilt over the centuries as needed. There were periods when certain sections weren't used and fell into ruin, while others were maintained because of their strategic position. The section at Juyongguan was restored in the 1990s following the Ming dynasty model.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260505_092351.jpg", "View from the wall" %}

I was so worried I wouldn't have enough time that I almost ran up and climbed 350 metres of elevation gain in 25 minutes instead of an hour. When I reached the top, a great panorama opened up in front of me – and most importantly, I could see the opposite hill and wondered whether I could reach it too. It looked very far away, but after sitting for a while and enjoying the view, I decided to try.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260505_093437.jpg", "The highest point I reached" %}

The descent was fast, but I had to be very careful because the steps were even steeper than on the way up, and the whole time I was worried whether I would have enough time. When I reached the top of the neighbouring hill, I realised it was much closer than I had thought. In about an hour and a half I covered a large part of the wall, and the rest of the time I simply enjoyed the views and the incredible feeling of walking along the stone dragon. The full loop is about 4.2 km, with a total ascent of about 500 m, counting all the repeated climbs.

If you have more time at Juyongguan, look for the Cloud Platform (Yuntai) in the valley. It is a marble arch from 1342, on which three stupas once stood. Its walls are carved with Buddhist texts in six different languages.
:::

::: curious
The Great Wall of China cannot be seen from space with the naked eye – that's a myth. Even China's first astronaut, Yang Liwei, said after his flight in 2003 that he hadn't seen it. In photos taken from orbit with powerful lenses it can be made out, but not with the unaided eye. And in the first complete survey, completed in 2012, all sections of the wall together came to 21,196 km – more than half the circumference of the Earth.

{% figure "images/china/Great_Wall_of_China_WEB.webp", "A photo of the wall from space – taken with a lens, not with the naked eye" %}

Still, the wall is truly majestic and well worth seeing. Be prepared, though, for lots and lots of steps – and of different heights at that.
:::

### The Summer Palace

After the long walk we went to the Summer Palace – the favourite place of Empress Dowager Cixi, one of the most iconic figures in Chinese history. We were told that after a great fire she diverted part of the money intended for modernising the navy to rebuild the palace, and as a result China lost the Opium Wars, which I'll talk about later.

::: note
**What it turned out to be:** the story about the diverted money has a real basis, but the wars are mixed up. The palace was burned by British and French troops in 1860, during the Second Opium War – i.e. the Opium Wars had already been lost before the rebuilding. Cixi rebuilt it from 1886 onwards using funds intended for the navy, and a few years later, in 1894–1895, China lost the war with Japan. The exact sums and how far they caused the defeat are still debated today.
:::

What impressed me most were the painted Long Corridor and the Marble Boat. The corridor is 728 metres long and decorated with over 14,000 paintings telling stories from Chinese mythology and literature, as well as scenes from everyday life. The imperial family could stroll along it by the lake, protected from the sun and the rain.

The Marble Boat is a stone pavilion in the shape of a boat, built in 1755. Cixi rebuilt it in 1893 into what we see today. The curious thing is that the upper part is wooden, painted to look like marble. There's a certain irony too – while China needed real ships, the palace got a ship that can never sail!

### The Temple of Heaven

The next day began with the Temple of Heaven (Tiantan) – one of the most important temples in China, built in 1420 under the Yongle Emperor of the Ming dynasty, the same ruler under whom the Forbidden City was built. Its most famous building, the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests, is built entirely of wood without a single metal nail. The current hall was actually rebuilt after 1889, when lightning set it on fire and it burned to the ground.

This was the place where the Chinese emperor symbolically communicated with Heaven. The emperors bore the title "Son of Heaven", and it was believed that their duty was to maintain harmony between Heaven and Earth. Every year the emperor performed special ceremonies to pray for rain, good weather and a rich harvest. If drought, famine or disasters struck, people could take them as a sign that the ruler had lost Heaven's favour.

### Traditional Chinese medicine hospital

As I mentioned above, the Chinese believe strongly in traditional medicine, and for many it is the best remedy. The interesting thing is that some of its herbs and approaches have been shown to have a healing effect, while many others have been shown to have none at all – and yet they continue to be prescribed.

After the Temple of Heaven we were taken to a traditional medicine hospital, where we were given a "diagnosis" – taking the pulse and examining the irises and the tongue. Science still can't confirm that diseases can be detected this way, but people believe in it and often go for such check-ups. We also got a foot massage, which was very pleasant, especially after walking the wall the day before.

What I didn't like was that after the diagnosis they started prescribing medicines of unclear effectiveness that supposedly cure everything – but for quite a lot of money. They have to be taken for a long time, and before people had even finished their first dose, there was already talk of how they would get the next ones. It also seemed strange to me because all sorts of unusual ingredients go into these medicines.

One of our guides even explained why there were so many sick people in China during COVID-19. According to him, the reason was that people turned en masse to traditional medicine, which was supposed to come up with a treatment for the new virus, and the old remedies didn't help.

::: note
**What it turned out to be:** China's biggest wave came at the end of 2022, right after the "zero-COVID" policy was abruptly lifted on 7 December 2022. The virus spread within weeks through a population with very little natural immunity and low vaccination rates among the elderly. Traditional medicine really is widely used, but it was not the cause of the wave.
:::

### The jade factory

China is famous for jade, and according to the Chinese, real jade, silk and tea can only be bought in China, while everything else in the world is an imitation – quite amusing to hear in the country famous for imitations.

::: curious
There's another irony too: today much of the green nephrite jade on the Chinese market is mined in Canada and Russia, and over 70% of the world's high-quality jadeite (the other stone called "jade") comes from Myanmar. The most prized in China remains the white nephrite from Hotan in Xinjiang.
:::

The shop was in a mall and was quite interesting, but what impressed me most was the "Ball of Generations" – a jade ball with many openings, inside which there are other, smaller balls that move freely, all carved from a single piece of stone. Each inner ball symbolises one generation of the family, and the whole composition – unity, happiness and the bond between generations. The Chinese also call it the "devil's ball", because it seems impossible that a human hand could have carved it.

The more inner balls there are and the more beautifully it is engraved, the more expensive it is, and it is believed to bring happiness to the home. There is also a variant with Chinese guardian lions on both sides of the ball – then the composition is called "Two Lions Playing with a Ball".

## Zhengzhou

After the jade shop we headed to the station to catch the high-speed train to Shaolin, but first we had to pass through Zhengzhou. This is one of the most ancient Chinese cities – about 3,500 years ago it was one of the capitals of the Shang dynasty – although today it looks extremely modern. We didn't get to explore the city, but I noticed that it is full of skyscrapers and must be a great place for a walk.

If you imagine the train as a maglev, you're wrong – from the outside it looks like an ordinary train, but it travels at "only" 300 km/h. Interestingly, there's none of the rattling we know so well from Bulgarian trains – inside it's quite quiet and very pleasant for resting after the long days so far. Before I left, I'd been told that at that speed everything outside becomes a blur, but that only applies to the trees right next to the train – everything further away looks just as it does from a normal train. Overall, these trains are what trains everywhere in the world should be: fast and reliable.

::: curious
China has the largest high-speed rail network in the world – at the end of 2025 it already exceeded 50,000 km, more than all other countries combined.
:::

On the way out of Beijing I noticed how many new buildings there were – entire complexes of high-rise blocks that looked empty. There were roads next to them, but neither people nor cars – like a ghost town. I had heard about this, but I didn't think it would surprise me so much when I saw it. Construction in China moves so fast that new neighbourhoods and even entire cities are built, initially empty, and gradually fill up. But if there's no work nearby, life in such places is hard.

I was also very surprised by what we were told about housing. The Chinese buy homes, but they don't really own them fully – they borrow them from the state for 70 years, after which this can be extended once by another 40 years for the heirs. The state remains the owner of the land and can at any time move people to another home of the same value and use the land for something else. I suppose many of the people in the new cities and neighbourhoods ended up there after such a procedure, or simply those were the only homes they could afford.

::: note
**What it turned out to be:** it's true that in Chinese cities the land belongs to the state, and the buyer gets the right to use it for 70 years (the home itself is theirs). The story about "once more for 40 years", however, isn't accurate. According to China's Civil Code, the right to the land under homes is renewed automatically when it expires, and 40 years is the maximum term for land used for commerce and tourism, not for housing. What will have to be paid on renewal has not yet been specified.
:::


## Dengfeng

You could say Dengfeng is the city of kung fu. It's small by Chinese standards – about 700,000 people – and we were told it has around 100 martial arts schools. (According to the data I found later, it's more like 40 to 60, but over 50,000 students study in them, and the largest one alone, Tagou, has tens of thousands.) So when someone tells you they trained at Shaolin, they most likely trained at one of these schools.

But if you think it's easy there, believe me – it isn't. From 6 in the morning we started hearing the boys' battle cries, and I had the feeling the whole city could hear them, but nobody seemed particularly bothered. It was strange to walk the streets surrounded everywhere by high walls with barbed wire on top – which, as we were told, isn't there to keep anyone out, but to keep the boys who are inside from getting out. What can I say – this is discipline: you go there for a few months or a few years, live as if in prison, and the only thing you do is train 10 hours a day to the point of complete exhaustion. This isn't the image from the films, where monks meditate on rocks in the mountains – it's more the modern way to build discipline. Those who make it through, though, can later become soldiers, police officers, actors or bodyguards.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260507_082456.jpg", "The boys’ training" %}

The way we ate was also very interesting – at round tables of about 10 people, whose central part rotates so that everyone can reach what they want. Since we're foreigners, the food wasn't very spicy, it was adapted to Western tastes, and there was even cutlery. If anyone thinks the food was scarce or not tasty, they're very wrong – it was great. It rarely happened that there was anything too exotic for us, like chicken feet. Most dishes were exotic but tasty, and quite close to what we eat in Chinese restaurants back home.

The rotating tabletop we all associate with Chinese restaurants is actually not such an old Chinese invention. Similar rotating trays existed in Europe as early as the 18th century, and they only became widespread in Chinese restaurants in the 1950s and 60s.

### The musical show "Shaolin Zen Ritual"

Before visiting the monastery itself, it's a very good idea to see the incredible show "Shaolin Zen Ritual". It has been performed since 2007 in a picturesque gorge in the Song Mountains, about 7 km from the monastery, and combines Zen Buddhist music on traditional instruments with martial arts demonstrations. The music is by the composer Tan Dun – the same one who wrote the score for "Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon" and won an Oscar for it. Sitting in front of the cliffs, watching hundreds of performers moving across the different parts of the stage, built full-size right up against the rocks, watching the lasers paint pictures and the cliffs seem to come alive with the lights at times – it's a truly unforgettable experience. I recommend it to anyone who has the chance, because the Chinese know how to impress with scale and detail.

### Shaolin Monastery

This is the place shown in countless films – almost every hero of the kung fu movies has some connection to it. Scenes of the monks training are shown constantly, and you start to think the monastery is huge, at least for several thousand people, and that it is somewhere very hard to reach. That's not quite how things are, though. You get there on an asphalt road, and if you expect fearsome cliffs, you'll be disappointed – inside there are quite well-kept little gardens. The mountains can be seen in the distance and one day I'll definitely have to visit them, but for now I had to make do with exploring the monastery.

The monastery was founded in 495 for the Indian monk Batuo. According to legend, a little later Bodhidharma arrived here too, founding Chan Buddhism (known in Japan as Zen). Today Shaolin is part of the UNESCO site "Historic Monuments of Dengfeng in 'The Centre of Heaven and Earth'".

### The Longmen Grottoes

In the afternoon we went to the Longmen Grottoes ("Dragon Gate") near the city of Luoyang. They're quite interesting and somewhat reminiscent of the Eagle Rocks near Ardino in Bulgaria, except that the niches contain figures of the Buddha. You walk along the Yi River and everywhere around you there are niches with sculptures – over 2,300 caves and niches in total and about 110,000 statues, carved between the end of the 5th and the middle of the 8th century. It's one of the finest examples of Chinese Buddhist art and a UNESCO site. In one place there are also huge sculptures that are simply incredible – the central Buddha is 17 metres tall.

Unfortunately, many of the statues are damaged. I thought this was mainly due to erosion, but later I found out that most of the damage was caused by people – at the beginning of the 20th century many heads and reliefs were cut off and sold to collectors and museums around the world.

## Xi'an

After another train journey we reached China's old capital – the city of Xi'an. ### The Big Wild Goose Pagoda

In the morning we started with one of the most important Buddhist monuments in China – the Big Wild Goose Pagoda, built in 652, under the Tang dynasty. I've watched many films about the Monkey King and his adventures, but the interesting thing is that they're based on the real journey of the monk Xuanzang.

In the 7th century he undertook an extremely dangerous journey from China to India that lasted about 17 years, and returned with over 600 Buddhist texts, which he began translating into Chinese. The pagoda was built precisely to keep them safe. His journey later inspired the famous Chinese novel "Journey to the West", in which the legendary Monkey King – Sun Wukong – appears.

### The Tomb of the First Emperor and the Terracotta Army

Before the tomb we visited a shop where they make modern terracotta warriors and other works of art. It was great – you could easily treat it as a museum.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260508_111038.jpg", "Hand-embroidered screens in the shop" %}

Qin Shi Huang is the man who unified China in 221 BC and became its first emperor. His tomb itself has still not been excavated and probably won't be any time soon. It's expected to contain untold treasures, but according to ancient chronicles the tomb is full of rivers of mercury – and measurements really do show elevated levels of mercury in the soil – so excavation would be very dangerous. The Chinese government has decided not to excavate the tomb until technology allows everything to be preserved for future generations.

The army was discovered completely by chance in 1974 by farmers digging a well. We were told that the pits contain over 8,000 life-size clay figures – soldiers, horses, chariots and more – arranged for a campaign and guarding the emperor's tomb and his path to the afterlife. In fact, 8,000 is the estimate for everything in the pits – about 2,000 figures have been excavated so far, and the rest still lie underground. I found it very interesting that in places the figures are covered with plastic film. The reason is that they were once painted, but as soon as air comes into contact with the paint, it begins to break down – the lacquer underneath wrinkles within seconds and flakes off within minutes. So they try to preserve as much of the colour as possible, and as soon as even a little paint appears, they cover it.

It's also interesting that almost no figure was found intact. The roofs of the pits collapsed, and under the weight of the earth the figures shattered, so they have to be put back together afterwards. In one spot there may be pieces of several sculptures, and the puzzle becomes very complicated.

While we were in the quarter, we also visited the Great Mosque, which is built entirely in Chinese style – without a dome and with a minaret that looks like a pagoda. ### The "Tang Dynasty" show

After the long day we went to an incredible show that was described to us as the Chinese "Moulin Rouge". "Tang Dynasty" is one of the most beautiful performances in Xi'an – with music, dance, lavish costumes and traditional instruments, it recreates life at the imperial court of about 1,300 years ago.

## Guilin

After this whole adventure, which wore us out, we flew south – to a part of China known for its beautiful nature and calm atmosphere. For us it was like a holiday – time to take in everything we had seen and experienced so far, a time when things happen more slowly and calmly. Over the next days I had the feeling we had travelled back in time, which was a pleasant change after the megacities.

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260509_091139.jpg", "The Chinese countryside from the plane window" %}

The name Guilin literally means "forest of osmanthus" – a fragrant tree whose small flowers have a sweet scent. The Chinese make tea, sweets and fragrant drinks from them, and in autumn, when the osmanthus blooms, the whole city smells of it.

{% figure "images/china/scale_1200.jpg", "Osmanthus flowers" %}

Guilin is famous for its incredible limestone hills, which rise almost vertically from the plain, for the Li River and for its old Chinese villages. The Chinese have a well-known saying:

> "The mountains and waters of Guilin are the most beautiful under Heaven."

Most people back home have never heard of this place, which is a pity, because for the Chinese it is one of their favourite places for holidays and walks.

### Reed Flute Cave

Our first stop was Reed Flute Cave. Its name comes from the reeds that grow around the entrance, from which flutes were once made. I knew nothing about it and was very pleasantly surprised. Inside there are many cave formations lit in different colours, some of which look like palaces, waterfalls and fantastic creatures.

On the cave walls alone there are over 70 old inscriptions – poems and travel notes written in ink. The oldest date from 792, from the time of the Tang dynasty. After that the cave was forgotten for centuries and was only rediscovered in the 1940s, when refugees from the Japanese troops hid in it.

### Towards Yangshuo

The next day we set off for the small town of Yangshuo, where we would spend the night. On the way our guide decided to tell us a bit more about China – but this time not from the point of view of the rich big cities, but from the point of view of someone who grew up on a farm.

### What we learned about work in China

Several times we asked how much holiday the Chinese get, and everyone told us something different, so I sat down to look into it. Here is the reality – although it also depends on the job, the employer and the province.

::: note
In China, paid annual leave depends on your total length of service:
- under 1 year of total service – usually no statutory right to leave;
- from 1 to 10 years – 5 days;
- from 10 to 20 years – 10 days;
- over 20 years – 15 days.

In addition, since 2025 the Chinese have 13 official public holidays a year (until then there were 11).
:::

The standard working week is 5 days of 8 hours, as in Bulgaria, and under the usual regime overtime is limited to 36 hours a month. The stories we have all heard are, in many cases, no longer true – or, if they do happen, they aren't entirely legal.

Before I went, I thought everyone worked under the "996" system I had heard about. The name comes from "9 a.m. to 9 p.m., 6 days a week". This regime became known in Chinese internet and tech companies.

In August 2021 China's Supreme People's Court, together with the Ministry of Labour, explicitly declared mandatory "996" illegal. Even so, the system hasn't completely disappeared – some people continue to work long hours because of competition, pressure from their employer or the desire to earn more money.

What really surprised me was that the Chinese are afraid of turning 35 – they even have a term known as the "curse of 35". We were told that as soon as someone turns 35, every day they start worrying whether they'll be called in for a talk with the boss and fired, after which it will be very hard for them to find a new job.

There are several reasons:
- Younger employees are often paid less.
- Employers believe young people accept long working hours more easily.
- People over 35 usually have more family responsibilities.
- Tech companies are constantly looking for new skills.
- Some employers prefer younger candidates even with similar qualifications.

There are documented cases of employees let go in restructurings right around this age, as well as job adverts with an upper age limit of 35. For a long time 35 was also the upper age limit for applying to the civil service – only in 2025 was it raised to 38 (and to 43 for people with a master's or doctoral degree).

It's important to say, however, that there is no law in China allowing automatic dismissal of people who turn 35 – the employer must have legal grounds to terminate the contract.

These things surprised me, because they're not what I imagined about China. The good thing is that things are gradually improving and the strange, unethical practices are disappearing.

### The Longji Rice Terraces

The next day we headed to the famous Longji rice terraces – the "Dragon's Backbone". I had seen them so many times in films that I imagined entire mountains completely cultivated this way. They turned out not to be quite so huge, but they were still very interesting. You definitely need good shoes, because you walk on village paths, and if you want to walk among the terraces themselves, be prepared for mud.

We were told that the terraces are so high up in the mountains because during different periods of Chinese history various peoples were persecuted and killed, and in search of refuge some people settled in the mountains. But there wasn't enough land there to feed themselves, so they started building these terraces, where they grew rice. Because of the high altitude, growing rice was difficult and huge areas were needed, so generation after generation the terraces grew into what they are today. They were started about 700 years ago, under the Yuan dynasty, and were only completed at the beginning of the Qing dynasty. There are several villages of the Zhuang and Yao peoples in the area – minorities with their own unique architecture, costumes and customs.

::: curious
The Yao women of the neighbouring village of Huangluo are famous for their extremely long hair – for some it is over 2 metres. The village has even entered the Guinness Book of Records as the "long-hair village".
:::

## Shanghai

After the pleasant trip through China's countryside, it was time to return to the big cities and visit one of the biggest – Shanghai. To get there we flew, crossing the country in a few hours to one of its economic centres. We were told that the city has a long history, full of quite unpleasant moments for the Chinese – especially during the Opium Wars.

::: note
The Opium Wars were two military conflicts between China and the Western powers – the First (1839–1842) against Britain and the Second (1856–1860) against Britain and France. The main cause was the British desire to keep the lucrative opium trade, which China was trying to ban because of mass addiction among the population. China lost both wars and was forced to open its ports to foreign trade, grant privileges to the Western states and cede Hong Kong to Britain.
:::

After the wars, Shanghai was divided into foreign concessions. We were told that Chinese people were forbidden to enter these territories, that if someone did enter they often didn't come out, and that many Chinese were sent to other countries to build railways and do other hard labour.

::: note
**What it turned out to be:** the story is more complicated. The Chinese not only entered the concessions, they made up the vast majority of their residents – according to the 1930 census, nearly a million Chinese lived in the International Settlement, while foreigners never made up more than about 4% of all of Shanghai. The Chinese were, however, second-class citizens: for a long time without their own representatives in the Settlement's government, and with access to places like the public parks only as servants. The sending of Chinese workers abroad is also real – they built railways in America, for example – but it isn't directly connected to the concessions.
:::

{% figure "images/china/IMG_20260513_102603.jpg", "A map of the foreign concessions in Shanghai" %}

What horrified me was the story that opposite the British concession there was a park with a sign reading "No dogs or Chinese allowed". When I read up on it, I found a photo of the sign from 1917, but it doesn't say exactly that.

{% figure "images/china/bi-s079.jpg", "A historical photograph of the rules for access to the public gardens in Shanghai, 1917–1928. University of Bristol archive." %}

According to the rules:

Rule No. 1: "The gardens are reserved for the Foreign Community."

Rule No. 4: "Dogs and bicycles are not admitted."

So one sign really did carry both a restriction excluding the Chinese and a ban on dogs – albeit in two separate points.

In the older rules from 1903, however, the Chinese are mentioned explicitly:

> No Chinese are admitted, except servants in attendance upon foreigners.

The park remained closed to most Chinese for decades, until the rules were changed in 1928. Today the site is Huangpu Park on the Bund, and the Monument to the People's Heroes stands in it.

{% figure "images/china/BxSAgrdP8L98OpAe8W6AxUwyKPOi1DlrXSvbTkTbU9A.jpg", "The entrance to the public garden in Shanghai around 1910–1911. Photo from the UK National Archives, published by Virtual Shanghai." %}

After this story I understand why the Chinese are a bit reserved when dealing with people from the West – they went through such discrimination and violation of human rights. Because of the long presence of Europeans, Shanghai has a very Western feel, it isn't like other Chinese cities, and it industrialised quickly, but at a very high price. It's believed that China's extremely strict attitude towards drugs also comes from this period.

In the 1990s construction began on Pudong – the new part of the city, where most of the skyscrapers we see today are. That's why they're clustered in one place: the old city was only on one bank of the river, while on the other, until 1990, there were mainly fields and warehouses.

### Zhujiajiao

In the morning we went to Zhujiajiao – an ancient water town about 50 km west of central Shanghai. Its history spans more than 1,700 years, and today it's called the "Venice of Shanghai" because of its beautiful canals, stone bridges and old houses.

Today the town looks like a romantic tourist attraction, but centuries ago it was a busy trading centre. The canals served as roads along which boats carried rice, cloth and other goods. Especially under the Ming and Qing dynasties, Zhujiajiao flourished thanks to trade, and the old houses by the water were once shops, warehouses and homes of wealthy merchants. Its most famous bridge, Fangsheng, was built in 1571 and is the largest five-arch stone bridge in the Shanghai area.

### Climbing the Oriental Pearl Tower

It's 468 metres tall and looks like a futuristic structure of giant pearls strung one above the other. It was completed in 1994, just as Shanghai was beginning to turn into one of the most modern megacities in the world, and until 2007 it was the tallest structure in China. The idea for its shape comes from a line by the Tang poet Bai Juyi, who compared the sound of the lute to "pearls, big and small, falling onto a jade plate".

### Yu Garden

Yu Garden is one of the most beautiful traditional Chinese gardens. It is located in the old centre of Shanghai and was created in 1559, under the Ming dynasty, by the official Pan Yunduan as a gift for his parents in their old age. It covers about 20,000 square metres and is full of pavilions, ponds, rock compositions, curving bridges and narrow paths that keep revealing new views. It's truly worth seeing, but to really enjoy it you need to sit down and give yourself over to the silence and calm – something that is hard to achieve today with so many tourists.

::: curious
Legend has it that someone reported to the emperor that the Pan family was building walls with dragons – a symbol reserved for the emperor alone – and was clearly plotting to fight for the throne. Pan found out in time and "trimmed" the dragons' claws so they wouldn't be imperial. And the famous "Exquisite Jade" rock isn't jade at all, but limestone from Lake Tai, 3.3 metres tall with 72 holes. If you light incense beneath it, the smoke comes out of all the holes at once.
:::

## Epilogue

A few months have passed since I visited China. My stay there was short – only 11 days – but so full of experiences and places that I needed time to realise where I had been. Now, as I look at the photos and write about the different places, I discover details I hadn't thought about at all on the spot. It's definitely different to read about a place and to be there and experience it, but each has its own charm.

When I came back and told friends about the trip, everyone asked me which place impressed me most. For me it was the evening in Chaoyang Park, when I met the young people who completely changed my idea of this country. Right after it come the Great Wall and the Terracotta Army. Now, looking at the photos, I'm overcome with nostalgia – it was a great adventure and I definitely want to go back to China. I hope by then there'll be robots on the streets and people will speak more English.

I wrote this article because after I came back, many people told me they wanted to read about my journey. Writing has never been my strong point and I never thought I'd get this far, but as you can see, I found the courage to describe it. There's no way to fit all the experiences into one article – there are so many things I couldn't include – but I hope you enjoyed it and will keep reading about my adventures around the world in the future.
